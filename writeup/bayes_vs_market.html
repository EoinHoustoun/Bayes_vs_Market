Bayes vs the Market

Does a hierarchical Bayesian goal model, fitted with MCMC, beat a classical Dixon-Coles model at forecasting Premier League matches? And can a tabular foundation model beat both? Tested walk-forward on five seasons against the closing betting market.

DRAFT for review, not published

The question

The closing price in a liquid betting market is one of the best football forecasts there is, so it is the right benchmark. I built two goal models with identical structure and fitted them two ways, then added a tabular foundation model as a third approach:

  1. Classical (maximum likelihood): team attack and defence ratings, home advantage, the Dixon-Coles low-score correction and a shared effect for promoted sides, with older matches down-weighted.
  2. Bayesian (hierarchical, MCMC): the same model, but team strengths share a learned spread, so a team with little data is pulled towards the league and its uncertainty is carried into every forecast. Sampled with NUTS in NumPyro.
  3. TabICL (tabular foundation model): a pre-trained model that predicts from a table in one forward pass with no training or tuning. Given the two models' forecasts, then also the market's.

Every forecast uses only matches played before it: each model is refit every 28 days on the previous three years and scored on the next 28 days, across 2021-22 to 2025-26.

The classical and Bayesian models finish level, about 0.023 behind the market. TabICL on the models' forecasts does no better than the models themselves. Given the market as well, it gets much closer but is still measurably worse than the market alone. It adds noise, not information.

The interesting part: my first Bayesian model lost

The first Bayesian run scored worse than the classical model, and clearly worse early in each season. The diagnosis was its forecasts were too cautious: its home-win probabilities barely varied between a mismatch and an even game.

How decisive each forecaster is

Spread (standard deviation) of the home-win probability across all matches. The market is the reference line.

  1. Cause: down-weighting old matches is standard, but in a Bayesian model it also shrinks the total evidence. Three years of matches counted as about 460, so the hierarchical prior pulled every team towards average.
  2. Fix (one pre-declared change, not a parameter sweep): keep the recency profile but rescale the weights to add up to the true number of matches.
  3. Result: significantly better than the first run, and level with the classical model. The classical model scored identically in both runs, which is the sanity check: maximum likelihood is unaffected by that rescaling, a Bayesian posterior is not.

TabICL goes the other way: it is more decisive than the market, and that overconfidence is what costs it.

Where Bayes earns its keep: promoted sides

Both models share one effect for teams in their first season after promotion. Maximum likelihood barely uses it, because it can push that weakness into each team's own rating. The Bayesian model pools it across every promoted side, and finds a large and growing penalty.

What I take from it

  1. The market is very hard to beat with results-only models. Any edge has to come from information the market prices slowly, not from a cleverer fit of the same goals data.
  2. Bayesian methods need their evidence counted honestly. A weighting choice that is harmless for maximum likelihood quietly changed the Bayesian answer.
  3. Where data is thin, pooling wins. The promoted-side effect is the clearest gain, and it matches what happened on the pitch: every promoted side went straight back down in 2023-24 and 2024-25.
  4. Foundation models are not magic on noisy, small tables. Zero-tuning TabICL matched hand-built models but did not beat them, and was overconfident.
